Agricultural Modes and Methods
Classification, historical development, ecological relationships and technological systems
I suggest developing Agricultural Modes and Methods as a structured research area within the Ecological Information project, with connections to the Ecological Indicators Inventory (EII) and Ecological Interaction Reference Knowledge Base (EIRKB).

An important starting point is distinguishing between agricultural modes, systems, methods, practices and technologies. These are related but not interchangeable.
1. A proposed classification
Level	Definition	Examples
Agricultural mode	The broad organisation of agricultural production and its relationship with land, labour and ecology	Subsistence, pastoral, industrial, regenerative
Agricultural system	The arrangement of agricultural activities within a particular landscape	Agroforestry, mixed farming, silvopasture, monoculture
Agricultural method	An established approach to managing agricultural production	Organic farming, biodynamic farming, conservation agriculture
Agricultural practice	A specific intervention or management technique	Mulching, intercropping, rotational grazing, cover cropping
Agricultural technology	Tools and infrastructure enabling agricultural activities	Irrigation, tractors, sensors, drones, robotic harvesting
Ecological mechanism	The biological or physical process affected by agricultural activity	Nitrogen fixation, nutrient cycling, mycorrhizal exchange
Ecological indicator	A measurable variable used to evaluate conditions or outcomes	Soil organic carbon, microbial diversity, infiltration rate


This structure would allow us to connect agricultural approaches to their ecological consequences, rather than simply compiling a catalogue of farming techniques.
2. Agricultural modes: an initial inventory
I propose beginning with eight broad families, recognising that some categories overlap and operate at different levels.
Family	Agricultural modes and approaches
1. Traditional and Indigenous	Shifting cultivation, terrace agriculture, oasis agriculture, traditional polycultures, transhumance, traditional Mediterranean dryland farming
2. Extensive and pastoral	Extensive grazing, nomadic pastoralism, ranching, silvopastoral systems
3. Intensive and industrial	Mechanised agriculture, industrial monoculture, intensive livestock production, controlled-environment agriculture
4. Ecological and organic	Organic agriculture, biodynamic agriculture, ecological farming, biological pest management
5. Regenerative and conservation	Regenerative agriculture, conservation agriculture, holistic grazing, soil-centred farming
6. Agroecological and diversified	Agroecology, agroforestry, permaculture, syntropic agriculture, food forests, integrated crop–livestock systems
7. Precision and computational	Precision agriculture, variable-rate farming, sensor-assisted irrigation, robotic agriculture, AI-supported farm management
8. Experimental and emerging	Microbial farming interventions, biointensive agriculture, cellular agriculture, climate-adaptive farming, digitally coordinated agricultural systems


These are provisional research families, not mutually exclusive scientific categories. For example, agroforestry is principally a land-use system, whereas agroecology is simultaneously a scientific discipline, a set of practices and a social movement.
3. A second classification: agricultural methods
An agricultural system can incorporate numerous methods.

I suggest classifying these according to the ecological or productive function they perform.
Functional category	Methods
Soil management	Tillage, no-till, mulching, composting, biochar application
Water management	Rainwater harvesting, contour cultivation, drip irrigation, keyline design
Nutrient management	Green manure, biological nitrogen fixation, nutrient recycling
Biodiversity management	Hedgerows, habitat corridors, companion planting, ecological refuges
Crop organisation	Crop rotation, intercropping, succession planting, polycultures
Tree integration	Alley cropping, silvopasture, forest gardens, windbreaks
Livestock integration	Rotational grazing, managed browsing, integrated crop–livestock systems
Pest management	Biological control, integrated pest management, pheromone disruption
Microbiome management	Microbial inoculation, mycorrhizal management, rhizosphere management
Landscape management	Terracing, erosion control, firebreak grazing, watershed restoration
Climate adaptation	Drought-tolerant cultivars, assisted diversification, microclimate management
Monitoring and automation	Remote sensing, soil sensors, robotic interventions, predictive modelling


4. Connecting agricultural methods to ecological information
For your wider research, I see particular value in establishing relationships between:

Agricultural method → Ecological mechanism → Measurable indicator → Ecological outcome → Place-specific suitability

For example:

Cover cropping
- Mechanisms: nutrient cycling, erosion reduction, soil carbon inputs.
- Indicators: soil organic carbon, microbial biomass, infiltration, vegetation cover.
- Potential outcomes: improved soil structure, reduced erosion, increased biodiversity.
- Context: suitability depends on rainfall, soil properties, crop competition and management.
Silvopasture
- Mechanisms: nutrient cycling, vegetation succession, microclimate regulation.
- Indicators: soil moisture, canopy cover, species diversity, livestock productivity.
- Potential outcomes: diversified production, habitat provision and improved thermal conditions.
- Context: particularly interesting for Mediterranean landscapes where grazing can also contribute to vegetation and fire-risk management.
This approach could eventually enable a GIS-based system to recommend agricultural methods according to the ecological characteristics of a specific location.
5. Relationship with the existing research projects
I suggest maintaining this as a distinct but interoperable inventory.

Agricultural Modes and Methods Inventory (AMMI) could contain:
- Agricultural modes and systems, including historical and geographical distributions.
- Agricultural methods and practices, including their technical requirements.
- Associated technologies and productive infrastructure.
- Ecological mechanisms and dependencies.
- Relationships to indicators in the EII.
- Scientific literature and historical references in the EIRKB.
- Geographical suitability, including climate analogues and future climate scenarios.
This would also support your research into food forests, agricultural history, regenerative industries and situated ecological technology.
6. Proposed research stages
I would organise the investigation into four stages.

Stage A — Agricultural Modes Inventory

Establish a comprehensive taxonomy of agricultural modes and systems, including historical origins, geographical distribution, theoretical foundations and relationships between approaches.

Stage B — Agricultural Methods Inventory

Catalogue agricultural practices, techniques and technologies, including their historical development, operational requirements and ecological mechanisms.

Stage C — Ecological and Geographical Relationships

Connect the inventory to ecological indicators, soils, climate, topography, species interactions and landscape characteristics. This would establish interoperability with the EII and GIS.

Stage D — Agricultural Suitability and Design

Develop a framework for comparing and selecting agricultural systems and methods for specific places, including climate-change adaptation, productive potential, biodiversity conservation and regenerative industrial opportunities.

It would allow us to ask not only which agricultural methods work in a particular landscape, but also why particular agricultural systems emerged there and whether alternative systems might now be more appropriate.

The intention is to develop a family of complementary knowledge bases, each maintaining its own research scope while sharing common principles for references, metadata, classification and database architecture.
1. Relationship between the databases
Database	Primary purpose
EIRKB	Ecological Interaction Reference Knowledge Base — scientific literature, concepts, theories, ecological relationships and associated knowledge
EII	Ecological Indicators Inventory — measurable ecological variables, indicators, methodologies and their interpretation
AMRKB	Agricultural Modes Reference Knowledge Base — agricultural history, modes, systems, methods, practices and technologies


The AMRKB will remain an independent project rather than becoming a subsection of the EIRKB.
2. Shared architectural principles
I propose that the AMRKB adopt the same underlying strategy as the EIRKB:
1. Markdown documentation — human-readable scope, architecture and reference material.
2. SQLite database — a structured and extensible knowledge base.
3. Persistent identifiers — stable references for agricultural systems, methods, technologies and sources.
4. Zotero interoperability — alignment with existing bibliographic standards without duplicating Zotero's functions.
5. GIS compatibility — geographical relationships, spatial data and QGIS integration.
6. Machine-readable exports — CSV, JSON and other interoperable formats.
7. Knowledge graph compatibility — relationships between agricultural approaches, ecological processes, technologies and places.
8. Version control — Git-based development, with version information maintained inside documents rather than filenames.
Importantly, the databases should share conventions without necessarily sharing identical data structures. Each will require specialised entities and relationships.
3. Particular scope of the AMRKB
The AMRKB should accommodate several dimensions of agricultural knowledge:

Historical: Origins, evolution, geographical diffusion and transformation of agricultural systems.

Conceptual: Agricultural philosophies, theoretical frameworks, traditions and schools of thought.

Technical: Methods, practices, tools, machinery and productive infrastructure.

Ecological: Relationships with soils, water, biodiversity, microbiomes, nutrient cycles and ecosystem functions.

Geographical: Climate, terrain, bioregions, cultural landscapes and place-specific suitability.

Socioeconomic: Labour, land tenure, productive organisation, agricultural economies and relationships with local industries.

These dimensions should eventually support comparisons between agricultural approaches across geographical regions and historical periods.
4. Development strategy
For now, I suggest proceeding in three steps:

Stage A — Scope and architecture: Define the AMRKB's boundaries, entities, classifications and relationships to the EIRKB and EII.

Stage B — Reference inventory: Develop a comprehensive catalogue of agricultural modes, methods, historical traditions, technologies and relevant scientific literature.

Stage C — Database implementation: Establish the SQLite schema, import the reference inventory and develop basic CLI tools for querying and maintaining the database.

We should avoid premature database implementation until the conceptual classifications are sufficiently developed.
